Complete HELOC Guide (2026)

A HELOC lets you borrow against your home's equity as a revolving line of credit. Here's everything you need to know before you apply.

What Is a HELOC?

A Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC) is a revolving credit line secured by your home. Unlike a home equity loan (a lump sum), a HELOC lets you draw funds as needed during a "draw period" — typically 10 years — and then repay them over 20 years.

Eligibility Requirements

  • Equity: Combined LTV (mortgage + HELOC) typically ≤ 85%
  • Credit score: 620+ minimum; 680+ preferred; 760+ for best rates
  • Debt-to-income (DTI): typically ≤ 43%
  • Income verification: pay stubs, tax returns, or W-2s
  • Home appraisal: lenders order their own appraisal
  • Property type: most HELOCs work on primary residences and second homes

How the Process Works

  1. Pre-qualify — get a soft-pull estimate from 2-3 lenders
  2. Apply — full application with income and asset docs (hard pull)
  3. Appraisal — lender orders an appraisal ($300–$700)
  4. Underwriting — 2-4 weeks typical
  5. Closing — sign documents; draw period begins
  6. Draw — use online transfer, checks, or card (varies by lender)

Draw Period vs. Repayment Period

Phase Duration Payment Type
Draw period 10 years (typical) Interest-only (common) or principal + interest
Repayment period 20 years (typical) Principal + interest (fully amortizing)

Important: At the end of the draw period, your minimum payment can jump significantly. Budget for this transition.

Common Pitfalls

1. Over-borrowing

Lenders may approve you for more than you need. A HELOC increases the risk of losing your home — borrow only what you'll actually use.

2. Variable rate exposure

HELOC rates are tied to the Prime Rate. If Prime rises from 8% to 10%, the interest on a $100,000 balance rises from $8,000 to $10,000 per year. Consider a fixed-rate option if your lender offers one.

3. Closing the line early

Many HELOCs have a 2-3 year early closure fee (typically $300–$500) to recoup closing costs. Read your agreement before you close.

4. Tax deduction assumptions

HELOC interest is deductible only if funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan. Do not assume deductibility for debt consolidation or personal expenses.

Alternatives to a HELOC

  • Cash-out refinance — replace your first mortgage with a larger one. Best if you can also lower your rate.
  • Home equity loan — fixed-rate lump sum. Best for one-time expenses with predictable amount.
  • Personal loan — unsecured, no home risk. Higher rates, but no foreclosure risk.
  • 0% APR credit cards — for short-term needs under ~18 months.

Is a HELOC Right for You?

Consider a HELOC if: you have significant equity, you need flexible access to funds, you can handle variable rates, and you plan to stay in the home long enough to recoup closing costs.

Skip a HELOC if: you're close to retirement and want stable payments, your income is uncertain, you might sell the home soon, or you can't comfortably handle a rate increase.
